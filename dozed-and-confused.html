The question

Does sleep predict GPA once you account for everything sleep competes with?

Sleep, screen time and study hours all draw from the same 24 hours. A bivariate correlation between sleep and grades can't tell you whether sleep is doing the work or standing in for the absence of something else.

The dataset records 3,000 students across fifteen fields: sleep hours, screen time, social media use, study hours, exercise, caffeine, self-reported stress and anxiety, GPA, plus flags for burnout and sleep-app use. The modeling question is narrow — estimate the association between average nightly sleep and GPA, then watch what happens to that estimate as the competing time-use variables enter the model.

The dataset is synthetic. That is stated up front because it changes what every number below is allowed to mean, and because the most interesting finding in this analysis turns out to be a property of the generator rather than of students.

The data

Unusually well behaved

No missing values across 45,000 cells. No duplicate rows, no duplicate student IDs. Every numeric field falls inside a plausible range, and the categorical splits are sensible — 1,681 undergraduates, 889 high school, 430 graduate; 68% report burnout; 34% use a sleep app.

For a dataset about self-reported sleep and mental health, that completeness is itself informative. Real survey instruments on this subject lose respondents in the anxiety items and the GPA field. Perfect completeness is the first hint of a generator.

Bivariate

Sleep tracks GPA, linearly

Across the observed range, each additional hour of sleep is associated with +0.204 GPA points (R² = 0.273). The binned means sit almost exactly on the fitted line.

The model

What survives the controls

Regressing GPA on all six time-use and consumption variables at once: R² = 0.356, adjusted R² = 0.354, n = 3,000.

The sleep coefficient falls from 0.204 to 0.140 — roughly a third of the bivariate estimate was screen time and social media wearing sleep's clothes. What remains is still the largest effect in the model by a wide margin.

Social media carries roughly three times the per-hour penalty of general screen time. Since social media hours are a subset of screen hours, the reading is that an hour reallocated from other screen use into social media costs about 0.035 GPA points on its own.

Mechanism

Stress absorbs about a quarter of it

Stress correlates with GPA at −0.558 — stronger than any behavioral variable in the dataset. Adding it to the model gives a possible pathway: sleep less, feel more stressed, perform worse.

Each hour of sleep is associated with −0.46 points of self-reported stress on the ten-point scale. Introducing stress as a covariate pulls the sleep coefficient from 0.140 down to 0.101 — a 28% attenuation — and lifts R² to 0.427.

What this does not establish

Attenuation is consistent with mediation, but it is equally consistent with stress and sleep sharing an unmeasured common cause, or with reverse causation: struggling students sleep badly. This is cross-sectional data with no temporal ordering, so the mediation story is a hypothesis the design cannot test. It is reported as a correlation pattern, not a mechanism.

The burnout flag tells the same story descriptively. Students reporting burnout average 7.16 hours of sleep against 7.96, 7.10 screen hours against 5.21, stress of 8.07 against 5.40, and GPA of 3.13 against 3.49.

Null results

Two things that did nothing

Null results are worth publishing when they are surprising, and both of these are.

Study hours do not predict GPA

The correlation between daily study hours and GPA is 0.002. In the full model the coefficient is −0.001 with p = 0.86. Students studying eight hours a day are indistinguishable from students studying one.

No plausible mechanism produces this in real students. It is the clearest evidence that study hours were generated independently of GPA rather than wired into it — a conclusion the variance inflation factors support, since study, exercise and caffeine all return a VIF of exactly 1.00, meaning they share no variance with any other predictor.

Sleep apps do nothing measurable

App users average 7.418 hours of sleep; non-users average 7.412. A two-sample t-test returns t = 0.18, p = 0.86. Whatever the app is doing, it is not adding sleep.

This one is at least plausible as a real finding. Self-selection would predict the opposite sign — people who already sleep badly are the ones who download a sleep tracker — so a null here could mean the app helps exactly enough to cancel out the selection. The synthetic data cannot distinguish that from the generator simply not connecting the two fields.

Diagnostics

The assumptions all pass, which is the problem

OLS returns numbers regardless. The classical linear model assumptions determine what those numbers are permitted to mean, so each was checked.

  1. 01Independent, identically distributed PassesOne row per student, no repeated IDs, no clustering variable such as school or cohort.
  2. 02Linear conditional expectation PassesBinned means fall on the fitted line; the sleep² term is not significant (p = 0.69). No curvature to model.
  3. 03No perfect collinearity PassesHighest VIF is 3.01 (screen time), well under the conventional threshold of 5.
  4. 04Homoskedasticity PassesBreusch–Pagan LM = 8.03, p = 0.24. Constant error variance, so classical standard errors are valid and robust SEs are unnecessary.
  5. 05Normally distributed errors PassesSkew −0.17, excess kurtosis −0.07, Shapiro–Wilk p = 0.08. Textbook normal.

Passing all five is the right outcome for a coursework exercise and the wrong outcome for believable data. Real self-reported GPA is bounded above at 4.0 and piles up there, which forces heteroskedasticity. Real sleep has a non-linear optimum. Real survey data has missingness that correlates with the thing being measured. None of that is present here.

Limitations

What these numbers are not

Synthetic data. These coefficients describe the generating process, not students. The 0.140 sleep estimate is a recovered parameter — evidence the model specification works, not evidence about sleep. Nothing here should be cited about actual academic performance.

Cross-sectional. Every variable is measured at one point in time, so no directional claim is available. Sleep and GPA are associated. Which one moves first is unanswerable by this design.

Self-report throughout. Sleep hours, screen time and GPA are all self-reported, and their measurement errors would correlate in real data — the same conscientiousness that produces accurate sleep logs produces accurate GPA recall. In synthetic data that shared error structure is absent, which is another reason the model looks cleaner than reality.

Unmeasured confounding. Household income, course load, employment status and diagnosed sleep disorder are all absent and would plausibly drive both sides of the relationship.

The finding

The honest headline is methodological. This dataset is a good instrument for demonstrating that a regression pipeline is specified correctly, that attenuation under controls behaves as theory predicts, and that assumption checking catches what it is supposed to catch. It is not evidence about sleep, and reporting it as though it were would be the exact failure the diagnostics section exists to prevent.

Reproduce it

Everything above, in R

Clone the repository, open Mental Health Analysis.Rproj, and run:

# install.packages(c("tidyverse", "car", "lmtest", "broom"))
library(tidyverse); library(car); library(lmtest); library(broom)

df <- read_csv("student_sleep_mental_health_2026.csv")

# Bivariate — 0.204 per hour, R2 = 0.273
m1 <- lm(gpa ~ avg_sleep_hours, data = df)

# Full model — sleep attenuates to 0.140, R2 = 0.356
m2 <- lm(gpa ~ avg_sleep_hours + screen_time_hours + social_media_hours +
              study_hours_per_day + exercise_hours_per_week +
              caffeine_drinks_per_day, data = df)
tidy(m2, conf.int = TRUE)

# Stress as covariate — 28% attenuation, R2 = 0.427
m3 <- update(m2, . ~ . + stress_level)

# Assumption checks
vif(m2)                                  # max 3.01
bptest(m2)                               # p = 0.24, homoskedastic
shapiro.test(sample(resid(m2), 500))     # p = 0.08, normal
anova(m2, update(m2, . ~ . + I(avg_sleep_hours^2)))  # p = 0.69, linear

# Sleep app null result — t = 0.18, p = 0.86
t.test(avg_sleep_hours ~ uses_sleep_app, data = df)
